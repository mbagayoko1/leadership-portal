# Prompt: Polish Your English module

A reusable prompt that writes a new, ongoing module, **Polish Your English**,
for the Leadership & Communication Development Programme. It helps participants
speak and write professional English that is clear, accurate and natural. The
practice videos come from the **Accurate English** YouTube channel
(<https://www.youtube.com/@AccurateEnglish/playlists>), an American English
pronunciation and fluency channel for professionals.

The module follows the same shape as every other module guide (see
[`module-guide.md`](module-guide.md) and [`vocabulary.md`](vocabulary.md)):
definition, key concepts, five worked examples, a step-by-step routine,
vocabulary and a self-check. It adds one thing: each concept points to the
Accurate English playlist to practise it with.

**How to use it:** copy everything below the line into Claude (or any capable
AI model), fill in the `Module` block, and send. Run it once in English and
once in French.

**Three decisions built into this prompt** (change them here if you disagree):

1. **Title.** "Polish English" can be read as the language of Poland, so the
   module is called **Polish Your English** (French: *Perfectionner son
   anglais*).
2. **Clarity, not accent replacement.** Accurate English teaches an American
   accent. Participants work around the world, so the goal is to be understood
   easily and to sound confident. The American model is the reference for
   practice, not a requirement. Participants keep their own accent.
3. **Link, never copy.** The guide links to the playlists. It does not
   transcribe, summarise lesson by lesson or reuse the channel's examples. All
   examples and drills are original.

---

You are an experienced English-language coach for international professionals
and an instructional designer. Write a learning guide for an ongoing module of
a 12-month leadership and communication development programme. The
participants are mid-career professionals in public service and international
development, working in countries and organisations around the world. Most
use English as a second or third language at work, in meetings, briefings,
emails and presentations. They are already capable professionals. They want
their English to be clearer, more accurate and more natural, so that people
remember their ideas rather than their mistakes.

## Module

- Module title: Polish Your English
- Phase and weeks: Ongoing (alongside all phases)
- Language of the guide: {English | French}
- Participant's first language (optional): {e.g. French, Spanish, Arabic,
  Portuguese, Swahili; leave blank for a mixed group}
- Main need (optional): {pronunciation | grammar accuracy | professional
  vocabulary | natural fluency | all}
- Module checkpoint: Record a two-minute work briefing at the start and again
  after eight weeks, and name three specific improvements.
- Length: {standard ≈ 1,800 words | full ≈ 2,800 words}

## Write the guide in this order

### 1. What this module is

- Two or three sentences: what the module covers (sounds, rhythm, accuracy,
  professional vocabulary and natural expressions) and why polished English
  matters for a leader's credibility and influence.
- Three learning outcomes starting with an observable verb, for example
  "Deliver a two-minute briefing with clear word stress…", "Correct your three
  most frequent grammar errors in emails…", "Use five professional expressions
  naturally in meetings…".
- One sentence stating the goal plainly: being understood easily and sounding
  confident, not losing your accent.

### 2. Key concepts

Six concepts, from the most basic to the most advanced:

1. **Intelligibility before accent**: how easily listeners understand you.
2. **Sounds that change meaning**: the vowels and consonants that most often
   cause misunderstanding (for example *ship/sheep*, *think/sink*,
   *law/low*).
3. **Word stress and sentence rhythm**: stressed syllables, content words
   versus function words, and the reduced vowel (schwa).
4. **Linking**: how words join in natural speech (for example *pick it up*
   sounding like "pi-ki-tup").
5. **Accuracy**: the small grammar errors that undermine credibility in
   meetings and emails (articles, tenses, prepositions, countable and
   uncountable nouns).
6. **Natural, professional expression**: collocations, idioms and phrases
   that sound fluent, and knowing when an idiom is too informal or too local
   for an international audience.

For each concept give:

- **What it means:** a plain definition in at most two sentences.
- **Why it matters here:** one sentence linked to participants' work.
- **Show it:** one short, original illustration. Show stress with capitals
  (deVELopment, PHOtograph, phoTOgraphy) and pronunciation with a simple
  respelling. Add IPA in brackets only when it helps.
- **Common mistake:** one sentence.
- **Practise with:** one or two playlists from the table, by name and link.

### 3. Worked examples

Five professional scenes, each focused on a different concept from section 2:
a briefing to a senior official, a partner video call, an email to a donor,
a conference presentation and a team meeting. Set them in five countries from
at least three regions (Africa, the Americas, Asia, Europe, the Middle East
and the Pacific), with speakers of five different first languages.

- Give each example a short title.
- Set the scene in two or three sentences, with fictional names and no real
  organisation.
- Show the **first attempt**: what the person said or wrote, with the problem
  marked (stress in capitals for spoken examples).
- Show the **polished version**.
- Explain in three or four bullets what changed and which concept each change
  uses.

### 4. Step-by-step practice routine

Six steps the participant can repeat every week, in about 15 minutes a day:

1. Record a baseline (a two-minute work briefing on your phone).
2. Choose one focus for the week from your recording.
3. Watch one video from the matching playlist and note the rule in your own
   words.
4. Practise with shadowing: listen, pause, repeat aloud with the same stress
   and rhythm.
5. Use the target in real work: one meeting, one call or one email.
6. Re-record and compare at the end of the week.

For each step give **Do**, **How** (with a script or sentence starter when
useful), **Time** and **Done when**. End with a weekly practice log the
participant can copy, in a fenced code block (columns: week, focus,
playlist, real-work use, what improved).

### 5. Vocabulary

Eight terms a participant needs to talk about their own English, for example
*intelligibility, word stress, schwa, linking, minimal pair, shadowing,
collocation, register*. Use the format in [`vocabulary.md`](vocabulary.md):
term and part of speech, a definition of 20 words or fewer, and a real-world
example with the term in **bold**, set in a different country each time. Add
a "Watch out" note only where learners often confuse the term.

### 6. Self-check

- Three reflection questions about the participant's own English at work.
- One sentence linking the guide to the module checkpoint.

## Rules

- **Clarity, not accent replacement.** Never describe an accent as wrong,
  heavy or something to remove. Say "clearer", "easier to follow" or "more
  natural". Treat American English as the practice model because the videos
  use it, and mention a British or international difference where it matters
  (for example the *t* in *water*, or the *r* after a vowel).
- **Spelling.** Write the guide in British English spelling (organisation,
  programme, behaviour), as in the rest of the portal. Keep playlist and video
  titles exactly as published.
- **Link, never copy.** Do not transcribe, quote or reuse the channel's lesson
  content or examples. Do not invent video titles. Credit the channel once in
  section 1 as "Accurate English (YouTube)".
- **First languages.** If a first language is given, include the errors its
  speakers commonly make, and point to the "Tips for Specific Native
  Languages" playlist. Use only well-known patterns. For French speakers,
  examples include the *th* sounds, a silent or added *h*, stress on the last
  syllable, and false friends such as *actually* (not *actuellement*) and
  *eventually* (not *éventuellement*). Do not make claims you are unsure of.
- **French version.** Write the explanations in natural French. Keep every
  English practice item in English, with a short French gloss where needed.
  Keep playlist titles in English.
- Plain language, active voice, short paragraphs and sentence-case headings.
- Never invent statistics, research findings or quotes.
- Use a balance of women and men, of regions and of first languages across
  examples. Avoid stereotypes about any country, language or accent.
- No organisation branding or logos.
- Output Markdown only, with no preamble before the first heading and no
  closing remarks after the self-check.

## Adding the module to the portal

When the guide is ready, it slots in next to Vocal Training as an ongoing
module:

- **Module entry:** `src/data.js` and `src/curriculumFr.js`, with id `eng`,
  `ph:0`, `wk:"Ongoing"` and the playlists above as its videos.
- **Guide:** the guide goes in `src/guides/en.js` and `fr.js`, with its five
  examples and eight vocabulary terms in the matching `examples-*.js` and
  `vocab-*.js` files. Add `"eng"` to `src/guides/ids.js`.
- **Checks:** run `npm run check`, which compares the English and French
  versions.
